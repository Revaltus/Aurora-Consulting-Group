Every so often, it is worth looking hard at your relationships. Your client list and vendor roster deserve the same look. Picture a business owner in Visalia with nine active clients who feels busy, tired, and oddly short on cash even though the calendar is full. The problem is rarely the whole book of business. It is usually two or three clients and four or five vendor contracts quietly eating the margin the others earn.

This post walks through how to fire unprofitable customers, how to renegotiate vendor contracts, and which tax details to check before you send any notice. The numbers are illustrative, but the pattern shows up constantly in the Central California businesses we work with.

## Measure what each client costs you to keep

Revenue on an invoice is not the same as cash in your account. A proper customer profitability analysis starts with what you billed, subtracts what you never collected, and then subtracts everything that client triggered: rework, refunds, extra supplies or subcontractors, and your own hours.

Here is what three clients might look like over a year, before overhead such as rent, insurance, and software:

| | Client A | Client B | Client C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amount billed | $22,200 | $20,400 | $24,000 |
| Amount collected | $22,200 | $17,000 | $22,000 |
| Rework, refunds, and direct costs | $1,300 | $4,200 | $2,800 |
| Your time (at $75/hour) | $750 (10 hrs) | $4,500 (60 hrs) | $1,875 (25 hrs) |
| Contribution | $20,150 | $8,300 | $17,325 |
| Share of billings kept | 91% | 41% | 72% |

Client B has the lowest billings of the three and still looks like a steady account on your invoice list. After three months of short-pays, a string of after-hours requests, and 60 hours of emailing and chasing, it keeps 41 cents of every billed dollar. Client A keeps 91 cents.

The time line is the one owners skip. If you value your hours at zero, Client B looks tolerable. Price them at what you would pay a part-time assistant or an outside specialist, and the picture changes quickly.

Your bookkeeping has to support this. That means each client or project tracked as its own class or customer in QuickBooks Online, direct costs coded to the right job, and payments matched to the right invoice. If you operate more than one entity, the same discipline applies entity by entity. Our outsourced accounting work usually starts with exactly this cleanup, because you cannot judge a relationship you cannot see.

## Set your breakup thresholds before you need them

Emotional decisions about clients go badly in both directions. You either tolerate a money-losing situation for years, or you react to one bad week. A written threshold takes the heat out.

Here are starting points we would discuss with a business owner, adjusted to your industry and margins:

- **Keep share below 60% of billings for two quarters in a row.** One bad quarter can be a one-off project overrun. Two is a pattern.
- **Three or more payments more than 10 days late in six months.** This is a cash flow problem for you, especially if payroll or a loan payment is due on the 1st.
- **Support or revision requests running at three times your client median.** Some are legitimate, and some are a client using you as free labor.
- **More than 25 hours of your own time in a year on one client's problems.** Past that point, the account is functioning as a part-time job.

None of these triggers an automatic exit. They trigger a conversation, which is the next step.

## Renegotiate before you end anything

The cheapest breakup is the one you never have to file paperwork for. Before you decide a client is a loss, test whether the terms can change.

At renewal, a rate increase of 5% to 8% on Client B's $20,400 would add roughly $1,000 to $1,600 a year. Pair it with autopay, a firm due date, and a written request process (requests through a ticket or portal rather than calls at 9 p.m.). Some clients take the new terms and the relationship improves. Others balk, and that tells you what you needed to know.

The same logic applies to any customer who generates most of your rush work or after-hours support: raise the price, tighten the terms, and watch what happens.

## End a client relationship the right way

If the numbers still do not work, you can exit, but this step needs care. Your agreement may set a notice period, a termination process, or obligations for work in progress, and the rules differ by contract and by industry. Talk to an attorney before sending anything. We are CPAs and Enrolled Agents, not your lawyer, and we would rather you spend an hour with one than a year fixing a notice mistake.

What we can help with is the money side of a graceful exit:

1. **Price a negotiated exit against the alternative.** If chasing a disputed balance would cost $4,000 in collection and legal fees plus two months of distraction, a settlement and a clean handoff can be the cheaper path.
2. **Document everything.** Keep the ledger, project log, and written notices in your shared digital folder. Clean records protect you in a dispute.
3. **Handle retainers and deposits by the book.** Prepaid amounts may need to be refunded or applied according to your agreement. Any amount you keep for unpaid invoices is income, so your bookkeeper needs to see it.
4. **Plan the replacement.** Filling the freed-up capacity with a client at market rates often recovers the lost margin faster than people expect.

## Know what the tax return does with a bad client

This is the part that surprises many owners. If your business reports income on the cash basis, an invoice a client never paid was never income. There is no bad debt deduction for it, and you simply do not report it. That makes the loss feel invisible on your return, even though it is very real in your bank account.

A few related points worth confirming with us before year-end:

- **Retainers or deposits applied to unpaid invoices** are generally income in the year you apply them.
- **Negotiated exit or settlement payments** are often deductible as an ordinary business expense, but if the payment ties to a sale or a major asset purchase, the treatment can change.
- **Costs to fix or redo work for a difficult client** can raise the expense versus capital asset question, so check with us before deciding how to book them.

The IRS publishes general guidance for small businesses at [IRS: Small Businesses and Self-Employed](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed), and it is worth a read. Your own facts decide the answer, though.

## Audit your vendors with the same standard

Clients are one side of the margin. Vendors are the other, and they are usually easier to fix because nobody has to move out.

List every recurring contract: software subscriptions, insurance, payroll and benefits providers, marketing or IT support, equipment and office leases, and your lender's terms if a refinance is on the table. For each, record the monthly cost, the renewal date, the notice window, and any early termination fee.

Then look for these:

| Contract | What to check | Realistic move |
|---|---|---|
| Marketing or outside services | Retainer versus results, auto-renewing scope | Ask for a smaller retainer or a scope tied to results, and get two competing bids |
| Equipment and supplies | Per-order pricing across locations or projects | Consolidate purchasing under one account and ask for a volume discount |
| Insurance | Separate policies by entity, outdated coverage amounts | Get quotes on a package, then compare coverage, not just premium |
| Software | Overlapping subscriptions, unused seats | Cancel the duplicate, move to annual billing only if you will keep it |

The [December business audit](/resources/the-december-business-admin-audit) covers the calendar side of this: put every notice deadline in your calendar 30 days ahead of the actual cutoff, because most auto-renewals trap people on a missed 60-day window.

## Decide when to terminate a vendor contract

When should you end a vendor contract instead of renegotiating? Terminate when the vendor has missed the same service standard twice after written notice, when a competing bid beats their price by more than 15% for the same scope, or when the contract's early termination fee is smaller than six months of the savings.

A quick example: your IT support provider charges $1,620 a month, and a competitor bids $1,350 for the same scope. That is $3,240 a year in savings. If the early termination fee is $800, you come out ahead in the first quarter.

Before you send notice, confirm which entity signed the contract and who holds the cancellation rights. In a multi-entity business, the wrong entity sending notice can void it. On the tax side, a termination fee is generally deductible as an ordinary expense in the year you pay it, but we confirm that against the underlying contract and entity.

## Common Questions

**How do I fire an unprofitable customer without burning a bridge?**

Start with a conversation about changed terms, such as a rate increase or tighter payment rules. If that fails, give written notice that follows your contract and local law, offer a clear timeline, and stay factual. For landlords in California, have an attorney review any tenant notice first.

**Can I deduct unpaid rent as a bad debt?**

Generally no, if you report rent on the cash basis. Rent you never received was never income, so there is nothing to write off. Accrual-basis taxpayers follow different rules, which is why your accounting method matters before you plan around a loss.

**How often should I renegotiate vendor contracts?**

Review each one at least 90 days before its renewal date, and run a full bid comparison every two to three years. A calendar reminder tied to the notice window protects you from silent auto-renewals.
