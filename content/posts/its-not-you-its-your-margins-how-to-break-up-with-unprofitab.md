Every February, the world tells you to look hard at your relationships. Your rental portfolio deserves the same look. Picture an investor in Visalia with nine single-family doors who feels busy, tired, and oddly short on cash even though every unit is occupied. The problem is rarely the whole portfolio. It is usually two or three tenants and four or five vendor contracts quietly eating the margin the other doors earn.

This post walks through how to fire unprofitable customers (for a landlord, that means tenants), how to renegotiate vendor contracts, and which tax details to check before you send any notice. The numbers are illustrative, but the pattern shows up constantly in Tulare County and Kings County portfolios.

## Measure what each door costs you to keep

Rent on a lease is not the same as rent in your account. A proper customer profitability analysis for a rental starts with what you billed, subtracts what you never collected, and then subtracts everything that tenant triggered: repairs, turnover, vacancy, and your own hours.

Here is what three doors might look like over a year, before mortgage interest, property tax, and insurance:

| | Door A | Door B | Door C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scheduled rent | $22,200 | $20,400 | $24,000 |
| Rent collected | $22,200 | $17,000 | $22,000 |
| Repairs and turn costs | $1,300 | $4,200 | $2,800 |
| Your time (at $75/hour) | $750 (10 hrs) | $4,500 (60 hrs) | $1,875 (25 hrs) |
| Contribution | $20,150 | $8,300 | $17,325 |
| Share of scheduled rent kept | 91% | 41% | 72% |

Door B has the second-lowest rent on paper and looks fine on a bank statement. After three months of short-pays, a string of after-hours repair calls, and 60 hours of texting and chasing, it keeps 41 cents of every scheduled dollar. Door A keeps 91 cents.

The time line is the one investors skip. If you value your hours at zero, Door B looks tolerable. Price them at what you would pay a property manager or a part-time assistant, and the picture changes quickly.

Your bookkeeping has to support this. That means each property tracked as its own class or location in QuickBooks Online, repairs coded to the right door, and rent receipts matched to the right tenant ledger. If you hold properties in more than one LLC, the same discipline applies entity by entity. Our [real estate investor accounting](/industries/real-estate-investors) work usually starts with exactly this cleanup, because you cannot judge a door you cannot see.

## Set your breakup thresholds before you need them

Emotional decisions about tenants go badly in both directions. You either tolerate a money-losing situation for years, or you react to one bad week. A written threshold takes the heat out.

Here are starting points we would discuss with an investor, adjusted to your market and financing:

- **Keep share below 60% of scheduled rent for two quarters in a row.** One bad quarter can be a roof leak. Two is a pattern.
- **Three or more payments more than 10 days late in six months.** This is a cash flow problem for you, especially if a loan payment is due on the 1st.
- **Repair requests running at three times your portfolio median.** Some are legitimate, and some are a tenant using you as a free handyman.
- **More than 25 hours of your time in a year.** Past that point, the door is functioning as a part-time job.

None of these triggers an automatic exit. They trigger a conversation, which is the next step.

## Renegotiate before you end anything

The cheapest breakup is the one you never have to file paperwork for. Before you decide a door is a loss, test whether the terms can change.

At renewal, a rent adjustment of $75 to $100 a month on Door B would add $900 to $1,200 a year. Pair it with autopay, a firm due date, and a written repair-request process (maintenance requests through a portal rather than calls at 9 p.m.). Some tenants take the new terms and the relationship improves. Others balk, and that tells you what you needed to know.

The same logic applies to commercial tenants and any short-term rental guests who generate most of your cleaning and damage costs: raise the price, tighten the terms, and watch what happens.

## End a tenancy the right way

If the numbers still do not work, you can exit, but in California this step needs care. Statewide tenant protections under AB 1482 cover many residential rentals, and cities and counties can add rules on top. Whether you can decline to renew, what notice you owe, and what you must pay in relocation assistance depend on the property and the tenant's history. Talk to a landlord-tenant attorney before sending anything. We are CPAs and Enrolled Agents, not your lawyer, and we would rather you spend an hour with one than a year fixing a notice mistake.

What we can help with is the money side of a graceful exit:

1. **Price a cash-for-keys offer against the alternative.** If an eviction would cost $4,000 in fees plus two months of vacancy on a $1,700 door, a $2,500 voluntary move-out can be the cheaper path.
2. **Document everything.** Keep the ledger, repair log, and written notices in your shared digital folder. Clean records protect you in a dispute.
3. **Handle the deposit by the book.** California sets strict deadlines for itemizing and returning deposits. Any amount you keep for unpaid rent is income, so your bookkeeper needs to see it.
4. **Plan the next tenant.** A re-rent after a repaint and a market-rate reset often recovers the lost margin faster than people expect.

## Know what the tax return does with a bad tenant

This is the part that surprises landlords. If you report rental income on the cash basis, as most individual investors do, rent a tenant never paid was never income. There is no bad debt deduction for it, and you simply do not report it. That makes the loss feel invisible on your return, even though it is very real in your bank account.

A few related points worth confirming with us before year-end:

- **Security deposits applied to unpaid rent** are generally income in the year you apply them.
- **Cash-for-keys payments** are often deductible as an ordinary rental expense, but if the payment ties to a major renovation or a sale, the treatment can change.
- **Repairs after a difficult tenant** raise the repair versus improvement question. Our guide to [repairs vs capital improvements](/resources/understanding-the-difference-between-repairs-and-capital-imp) shows how to decide what you can deduct now and what must be depreciated.

The IRS publishes general guidance for landlords and small businesses at [IRS: Small Businesses and Self-Employed](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed), and it is worth a read. Your own facts decide the answer, though.

## Audit your vendors with the same standard

Tenants are one side of the margin. Vendors are the other, and they are usually easier to fix because nobody has to move out.

List every recurring contract: property manager, landscaper, pest control, HVAC service plan, insurance, accounting and rent-collection software, and your lender's terms if a refinance is on the table. For each, record the monthly cost, the renewal date, the notice window, and any early termination fee.

Then look for these:

| Contract | What to check | Realistic move |
|---|---|---|
| Property manager | Fee on collected rent, leasing fees, markups on repairs | Ask for 8% instead of 10% on a nine-door portfolio, worth about $4,000 on $199,800 in annual rent |
| Landscaping | Per-visit rate by property | Bundle all nine addresses under one monthly invoice and ask for a multi-property discount |
| Insurance | Separate policies by LLC, outdated replacement values | Get quotes on a package, then compare coverage, not just premium |
| Software | Overlapping subscriptions, unused seats | Cancel the duplicate, move to annual billing only if you will keep it |

The [December business audit](/resources/the-december-business-admin-audit) covers the calendar side of this: put every notice deadline in your calendar 30 days ahead of the actual cutoff, because most auto-renewals trap people on a missed 60-day window.

## Decide when to terminate a vendor contract

When should you end a vendor contract instead of renegotiating? Terminate when the vendor has missed the same service standard twice after written notice, when a competing bid beats their price by more than 15% for the same scope, or when the contract's early termination fee is smaller than six months of the savings.

A quick example: your landscaper charges $1,620 a month across nine properties, and a competitor bids $1,350. That is $3,240 a year in savings. If the early termination fee is $800, you come out ahead in the first quarter.

Before you send notice, confirm which entity signed the contract and who holds the cancellation rights. In a multi-LLC portfolio, the wrong entity sending notice can void it. On the tax side, a termination fee is generally deductible as an ordinary expense in the year you pay it, but we confirm that against the underlying property and entity.

## Common Questions

**How do I fire an unprofitable customer without burning a bridge?**

Start with a conversation about changed terms, such as a rate increase or tighter payment rules. If that fails, give written notice that follows your contract and local law, offer a clear timeline, and stay factual. For landlords in California, have an attorney review any tenant notice first.

**Can I deduct unpaid rent as a bad debt?**

Generally no, if you report rent on the cash basis. Rent you never received was never income, so there is nothing to write off. Accrual-basis taxpayers follow different rules, which is why your accounting method matters before you plan around a loss.

**How often should I renegotiate vendor contracts?**

Review each one at least 90 days before its renewal date, and run a full bid comparison every two to three years. A calendar reminder tied to the notice window protects you from silent auto-renewals.
