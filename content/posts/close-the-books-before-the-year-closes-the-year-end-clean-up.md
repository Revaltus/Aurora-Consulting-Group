Most real estate investors we talk to in Visalia are not behind on their taxes. They are behind on their bookkeeping, and that is the part that quietly inflates the tax prep invoice. When a preparer opens the file in January and finds eleven months of uncategorized transactions, a mortgage payment coded entirely to interest, and a Venmo payment to a handyman nobody can identify, the hours go to detective work instead of tax planning.

The fix is cheaper in Q4 than at any other time of year. Your memory of what happened at the duplex on the west side of town is still fresh, the bank feeds are open, and December 31 is still ahead of you, which means a few decisions can still change your return. This year-end bookkeeping checklist for taxes is written for rental owners in Visalia and across Tulare, Kings, Fresno, and Kern counties.

## Why messy books raise your tax prep bill

Tax preparers bill for time and complexity. A clean set of books lets a preparer pull numbers straight onto Schedule E or an entity return. A messy set forces them to rebuild the year first, and you pay for that rebuild at preparation rates.

Picture an investor with four rentals in Visalia, one in Hanford, and a personal checking account that has paid for two of the mortgages. Nothing about that portfolio is unusual. But if every transaction sits in one uncategorized pile, someone has to sort roughly a year of activity property by property before a single deduction can be claimed. That sorting is the expensive part, and you can do most of it yourself, or hand it to your bookkeeper, for far less.

The [IRS recordkeeping guidance for small businesses](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/recordkeeping) says plainly that you need records that support your income and deductions. A reconciled ledger is that support. If an examiner ever asks about a Fresno County rental, you want to answer from the books, not from memory.

## Reconcile every account, including the ones you forgot about

Reconciling means matching your books to the bank or lender statement, line by line, until the ending balances agree. It sounds basic. It is also where most errors surface.

Start with the obvious accounts, then keep going:

- Operating checking for each LLC or property
- Separate security deposit accounts (California has rules about how deposits are handled, so these should never blend into operating cash)
- Credit cards used for supplies, travel to out-of-town properties, or contractor payments
- Mortgage and HELOC accounts, reconciled to the lender's year-end statement
- Payment apps like Venmo, Zelle, or PayPal that tenants or vendors use
- Any account you opened mid-year for a new acquisition

Work through the statements through December 31 as soon as they post, ideally in the first week of January. Do not wait for the last month to start. Reconcile October and November now, and December becomes a small job instead of a big one. If you are working toward a goal of reconciling bank accounts before tax season, the real win is finishing eleven months before the clock starts.

Watch for the signs of an unreconciled account: a negative cash balance in your software, a bank balance that is thousands off from your books, or old uncleared checks from last spring. Each one has a cause, and each cause usually affects a deduction.

## Separate personal and business expenses before December 31

Commingling is the most common problem we see with newer investors, and it carries a cost beyond tax prep. If you own properties inside an LLC and pay LLC expenses from a personal account, or the reverse, you weaken the separation that the LLC exists to provide. California also charges an $800 minimum annual franchise tax on LLCs, so the structure has a real price. You want to be getting the protection you are paying for.

Here is a practical way to clean it up in an afternoon:

1. Pull a list of every transaction between your personal accounts and your rental accounts this year.
2. Label each one as an owner contribution (you put money in), an owner draw or distribution (you took money out), or a reimbursement for a specific expense.
3. Attach a note for any reimbursement that names the property and the invoice.
4. Move any remaining personal purchases, such as groceries or a family dinner, out of expense categories and into owner draws.
5. Decide which account pays for what from here on, and stick to it in 2025 and beyond.

If you own a second home that you sometimes rent out, the personal-use days change how expenses are split. Our post on [turning a second home into a tax win](/resources/how-to-turn-a-second-home-into-a-tax-win) walks through the 14-day rule and the personal-use thresholds. Track those days now, while you can still reconstruct the calendar.

## Fix miscategorized transactions that change your deductions

Categorization errors are not cosmetic for real estate investors. A repair is deductible this year. An improvement is capitalized and depreciated, typically over 27.5 years for residential rental property and 39 years for commercial. Land is not depreciable at all. Put a $14,000 roof replacement in the wrong bucket and you have misstated your income for the year and handed the preparer a question to resolve.

The table below shows the mistakes we correct most often in rental books.

| Transaction | Common mistake | Better treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Mortgage payment | Entire payment coded to interest | Split into interest (deductible), principal (reduces the loan), and escrow (taxes and insurance) |
| New roof or HVAC replacement | Coded to repairs | Usually capitalized and depreciated; routine patching stays in repairs |
| Tenant security deposit | Booked as rental income | Held as a liability until it is applied or forfeited |
| Transfer to your personal account | Coded as an expense | Owner draw or distribution |
| Closing costs on a purchase | Expensed when paid | Added to basis, with the land and building allocated |
| Property tax and insurance paid through escrow | Missing from the books | Recorded from the lender's annual escrow statement and Form 1098 |
| Payments to a handyman or painter | Lumped into "Contractors" with no vendor name | Tracked by vendor, with a W-9 on file for 1099 reporting |

If you want a quick filter, run a report of every transaction coded to "Uncategorized," "Ask My Accountant," or "Miscellaneous." Those three labels are where unresolved decisions go to hide. Anything over a few hundred dollars deserves a real answer before year-end.

The repair-versus-improvement line also has a useful tool: the de minimis safe harbor election, which lets many owners deduct items under $2,500 per invoice instead of capitalizing them. It has to be elected on the return, and it only works if your books show invoice-level detail. This is one more reason to fix miscategorized transactions now rather than in a rush on April 14.

## Tie out the balance sheet items preparers always ask about

Income and expense accounts get the attention, but the balance sheet is where multi-property investors lose the most time. Before the year closes, confirm each of the following.

**Loan balances.** Your books should match the lender's December statement for each mortgage. If they do not, principal payments have probably been posted as expenses.

**Security deposits.** The liability on your books should equal the deposits you actually hold, tenant by tenant.

**Fixed assets and depreciation.** Every property placed in service needs a basis, an in-service date, and a depreciation schedule. If you bought something in 2025, add it now. If you are considering a cost segregation study on a larger acquisition, the decision is easier when your basis and closing documents are already organized.

**Due to and from other entities.** If one LLC paid another's expenses, that is an intercompany balance and should be recorded as one. Investors with several LLCs across more than one state tend to accumulate these without noticing, and they are painful to reconstruct a year later.

**Contractor payments.** Collect W-9s from every vendor you paid in 2025, and total what you paid each one. Form 1099-NEC filings are due January 31, so missing paperwork in December becomes a penalty risk in February. Confirm the current filing threshold with your preparer, because it has been changing.

## A realistic Q4 timeline

You do not need to do all of this in one weekend. Spread across the quarter, it looks like this:

| When | What to finish |
|---|---|
| October | Reconcile January through September for every account; clear the Uncategorized list |
| November | Separate personal transactions; collect W-9s; update depreciation schedule for any 2025 purchases |
| First two weeks of December | Review profit by property; decide on any repairs, purchases, or estimated payments that should land before December 31 |
| Early January | Reconcile December statements; send your preparer a final package with loan statements, 1098s, and escrow statements |

The December review matters most. With a reconciled ledger, you can see profit by property and decide whether to pay a repair invoice before the year ends, make a fourth-quarter estimated payment, or fund a retirement account. Without one, you are guessing. That is the difference between tax preparation, which records the past, and [tax planning and strategy](/services/tax-planning-strategy), which still has time to change the result.

## Common Questions

**What should be on a year-end bookkeeping checklist for a rental property owner?**

Reconcile every bank, credit card, and loan account to its December statement. Separate personal spending from rental activity. Clear all uncategorized transactions, split mortgage payments into interest, principal, and escrow, update your depreciation schedule, and collect W-9s from every vendor you paid during the year.

**Does cleaning up my books really lower tax prep fees?**

Usually, yes. Preparers charge for the time it takes to sort and verify your numbers. If your accounts are reconciled and categorized by property, that sorting disappears. The savings are largest for owners with several properties, multiple LLCs, or a year of mixed personal and rental transactions.

**When should I start reconciling before tax season?**

Start in October, not January. Reconcile the first nine months, then handle November and December as statements arrive. That leaves the first half of December free to review profit by property and make decisions that still count for the current tax year.

**Can I fix miscategorized transactions after December 31?**

Yes, you can still correct the books for the year before your return is filed. What you lose is the chance to act on what you find. Deciding to pay a repair invoice or make an estimated payment has to happen by December 31 to count.
