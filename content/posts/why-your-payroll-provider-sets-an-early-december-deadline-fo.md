That email from your payroll provider usually lands around Thanksgiving: final changes for the year are due in early December. If you run a business in Visalia, employ a handful of people, or pay a few contractors through the year, it is easy to file that notice under "deal with it later." Later is exactly when the trouble starts. A bonus you meant to pay in December gets dated January. An owner's health insurance never reaches the W-2. A truck that doubles as the family vehicle shows up in nobody's wages.

The deadline is not red tape. It is the window your provider needs to get your W-2s and 1099s right the first time, and it works in your favor once you know what belongs inside it.

## Why the cutoff falls in early December

Your payroll provider has more to do in December than run your last paycheck. It has to deposit payroll taxes on schedule, reconcile the fourth-quarter federal return (Form 941) and California's quarterly filings, and then build every W-2 and 1099-NEC. Those forms are due to employees and contractors by January 31, and a provider serving thousands of employers cannot rebuild yours on the last day of January.

The early-December date is when the provider locks the year's inputs. Anything you tell them before that goes into the normal workflow. Anything after it becomes a manual adjustment, and manual adjustments are where corrected forms come from.

Most payroll platforms follow the same pattern. As a [Gusto Partner](/contact), we see these notices every year, and the cutoff dates differ by a few days from one provider to the next. Check yours. The first or second week of December is typical.

The cost of missing it is practical, not dramatic. You file a W-2c to fix an error, your employee waits on a corrected form before filing their own return, and the IRS penalties for incorrect or late information returns grow the longer a mistake sits. The [IRS page on Form W-2](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-2) outlines the filing rules and where corrections fit in.

## Four items business owners tend to miss

Most of the owners we work with do not have big payrolls. They have small, interesting ones: an S-corp with the owner on payroll, a few full-time employees, a part-timer or two, maybe a bookkeeper working remotely from another state. Small payrolls hide the same four problems every year.

### Bonuses and their timing

Say you want to reward your operations manager with an $8,000 bonus for helping the business hit its biggest revenue goal yet. If that bonus is paid through payroll by December 31, it is this year's wage. If it runs after your provider's cutoff and lands in a January pay period, it is next year's wage, taxed in a different year than you planned.

That shift matters for your deduction too. The bonus also needs withholding. Federal supplemental withholding is a flat 22 percent on bonuses up to $1 million, and California applies its own supplemental rate. Tell your provider whether the bonus is meant to be gross or net, because "I want her to take home $8,000" is a very different payroll entry.

Performance bonuses have a timing trap of their own. If a December sale or project completion is going to trigger a bonus, decide the amount before the cutoff, even if the final payment has not come in yet. Your provider can often schedule the payment for a late-December date, which beats scrambling after the deadline.

### Company vehicle use

A pickup that hauls tools to a job site and then takes the kids to practice is a taxable fringe benefit to whoever drives it, whether that is an employee or you as an S-corp owner. The personal portion counts as wages. The [IRS Publication 15-B](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-publication-15-b) lays out the valuation methods, including cents-per-mile and the annual lease value rule.

A few thousand personal miles is enough to add a four-figure amount to someone's W-2. A mileage log kept through the year makes this a ten-minute calculation in early December. Without a log, it becomes an estimate, and estimates attract questions.

### S-corp owner health insurance

If your company is an S-corp and you own more than 2 percent, health insurance premiums the company pays for you are reported as wages in Box 1 of your W-2. They are generally not subject to Social Security and Medicare tax (Boxes 3 and 5) when paid under a qualifying plan. You then claim the self-employed health insurance deduction on your personal return.

Here is where it goes wrong. Suppose the company pays $1,150 a month for your family's plan. That is $13,800 a year. If payroll never records it, your W-2 is understated, your personal deduction has no matching wage to support it, and a W-2c is the likely fix. The remedy is easy in early December: give your provider the year-to-date premium total and the plan details so the entry is made once and made correctly.

### Employee data and on-site housing

This category is the quiet one. Address changes, a name change after a marriage, a corrected Social Security number, or a new W-4 all need to be in before W-2s are built. A name that does not match Social Security records is one of the most common reasons W-2s bounce.

Employer-provided housing is a special case, such as an on-site manager who lives in a unit you own or lease. Free or discounted housing can be tax-free in narrow situations where it is for your convenience, required for the job, and located on your business premises. Outside that test, the value of the rent is wages. Settle which situation you are in before December, and make sure your provider knows.

If you have an employee working remotely from Arizona or Texas while your business is based in California, flag it now. State withholding and registration follow where the work is performed, which can add a layer of compliance you will want to plan for.

## A year-end payroll checklist for small business owners

Here is the whole list in one place. Print it, or paste it into an email to your provider.

| Item | Why it matters | What to send your provider |
|---|---|---|
| Bonuses | Sets the tax year and withholding | Amount, gross or net, pay date by December 31 |
| Company vehicle personal use | Taxable fringe benefit added to wages | Personal miles or the valuation method used |
| S-corp owner health insurance | Reported in W-2 Box 1 for 2% shareholders | Year-to-date premiums and plan details |
| Employer-provided housing | Taxable unless it meets the exclusion test | Unit rent value and your conclusion |
| Employee data | Prevents mismatched W-2s | New addresses, names, SSNs, and W-4s |
| Remote or out-of-state staff | Triggers state withholding | Work location and start date |
| Contractor details | Needed for 1099-NEC filing | W-9s on file for every 1099 vendor |

The [IRS employment tax page for small businesses](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/employment-taxes) is a good reference if you want the underlying rules in the government's own words.

## Do not forget the contractors

Many Visalia business owners pay more contractors than employees: consultants, designers, tradespeople, seasonal help. Payroll providers often handle 1099-NEC filing for you, but only from the data you give them. A W-9 collected in January, after the work was done in March, tends to arrive with a missing Tax ID and a vendor who has stopped returning your calls.

In early December, pull your vendor list from the year, spot anyone paid by check or ACH outside your payroll system, and confirm you have a W-9 for each. Payments made by credit card are typically reported by the card processor instead, so those usually do not belong on your 1099s. Sorting that out in the first week of December is a lot easier than in the last week of January.

## A simple timeline to work backward from

The first week of November is a good time to start, because the provider's date is closer than it feels.

1. **Early November:** Review your vendor list, collect missing W-9s, and decide on any year-end bonuses.
2. **Mid November:** Pull your mileage logs and total your health insurance premiums for the year.
3. **Late November:** Send your provider the full change list, a week before their stated deadline.
4. **Early December:** Confirm in writing that each change has been entered, and review a draft payroll register.
5. **Mid to late December:** Run final payrolls, with bonuses dated before December 31.

If you have a 401(k) with a safe harbor match, the November planning window is even tighter. Our post on [safe harbor 401(k) timing](/resources/safe-harbor-safe-timing-why-your-401-k-decisions-happen-in-n) explains which decisions need to happen before December 1.

## Common Questions

**Is the early-December deadline a legal requirement?**

No. It is your payroll provider's internal cutoff, not an IRS date. The legal deadlines are January 31 for W-2s and 1099-NECs. The provider sets the earlier date so it can process, check, and file everything on time, and changes sent after it often cost extra time or a correction.

**Do I have to put my own health insurance on my W-2 if I own an S-corp?**

Yes, if you own more than 2 percent. Premiums the company pays for you are included in Box 1 wages, generally without Social Security or Medicare tax. You then deduct them on your personal return. Skipping the W-2 entry can put that deduction at risk and often leads to a W-2c.

**What if I miss the deadline?**

Ask your provider what they can still do, and contact your CPA right away. Many errors can be fixed with a manual adjustment or a W-2c, but fixing them costs more than getting them right in November. The goal is a clean January, not a perfect December.
