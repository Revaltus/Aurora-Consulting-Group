Ask most business owners about cost segregation, and you will usually hear the same answer: that's for people who own buildings. It is a reasonable guess. It is also wrong often enough to cost real money. Tenants who pay for their own build-outs can often speed up their deductions, and investors who own the building can do the same on their side of the lease. The difference between getting this right and missing it comes down to a few classification rules, a handful of dates, and what your lease says about who pays for what.

This guide walks through how a cost segregation study works, how it applies to owners and renters, where it breaks down, and how to decide whether the fee is worth it. Tax rules and bonus depreciation percentages change, so treat the numbers here as a framework and confirm current law before you act.

## How a cost segregation study speeds up depreciation

When you buy or build a commercial building, the default is to depreciate the whole cost, excluding land, over 39 years. Residential rental property uses 27.5 years. A cost segregation study is an engineering-based breakdown of that cost into components that the tax code lets you depreciate faster.

The study does not create new deductions. It moves the same total deduction into earlier years, which is worth money because a dollar of tax saved this year can be reinvested this year.

| Recovery period | Typical components | Bonus depreciation eligible |
|---|---|---|
| 5 years | Carpeting, specialty lighting, dedicated electrical and cabling for equipment, certain cabinetry and millwork | Yes |
| 7 years | Office furniture, fixtures, built-in equipment | Yes |
| 15 years | Parking lots, paving, landscaping, site lighting, drainage, and qualified improvement property | Yes |
| 27.5 or 39 years | Structure, roof, walls, standard HVAC, base-building plumbing and electrical | No |

[IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) lays out the recovery periods, conventions, and the Section 179 rules behind this table. It is dense reading, but it is the source your CPA and the engineer will both work from.

## What it looks like for a Visalia building owner

Here is a simple scenario for a building owner. Say you buy a 12,000-square-foot commercial office building in Visalia for $2.4 million. The land is worth $400,000, so the depreciable basis is $2.0 million. On a straight-line 39-year schedule, that is roughly $51,000 of depreciation a year.

Now suppose an engineering study reclassifies 22 percent of the depreciable basis, or $440,000. Of that, $120,000 lands in 5- and 7-year property, and $320,000 lands in 15-year land improvements such as the parking lot, lighting, and site work.

With 100 percent bonus depreciation available, that entire $440,000 can be deducted in year one. At a combined 35 percent tax rate, that is about $154,000 in tax savings in the first year. Without the study, the same $440,000 would have produced about $11,300 of annual depreciation, or roughly $3,900 of annual tax savings.

The total deduction over the life of the building is the same either way. What changes is when you get it. That timing is the whole point, and it is also why the strategy comes with trade-offs on sale, which we cover below.

Owners who bought years ago are not shut out. A study done on a building already in service can be handled with a change in accounting method (Form 3115), which captures missed depreciation in the current year without amending old returns.

## Why renters can benefit too

The assumption that tenants cannot use cost segregation rests on a true fact: you cannot depreciate a building you do not own. But tenants who pay for improvements own the cost of those improvements for tax purposes, and those costs are depreciable by the tenant.

Consider a Visalia business that leases 4,000 square feet in a building it does not own, and spends $320,000 on a build-out. A careful breakdown of the contractor's costs might look like this:

- **$85,000** in 5- and 7-year property: furniture, specialty cabinetry, dedicated electrical and data cabling, built-in equipment.
- **$205,000** in qualified improvement property: interior walls, ceilings, flooring, standard lighting, and interior finishes.
- **$30,000** in 39-year property: something like an enlargement or structural work that does not qualify for faster treatment.

Under current rules, the first two buckets, $290,000 in total, are eligible for bonus depreciation. Without that breakdown, a bookkeeper might capitalize the whole $320,000 as a single leasehold improvement and miss the faster treatment on part of it.

A tenant-side exercise does not always need a full engineering study. For build-outs under roughly $250,000, itemized contractor invoices and a CPA's analysis often capture most of the value. Above that, or where the invoices are lump-sum and unclear, a formal study begins to earn its fee.

## Qualified improvement property and Section 179

Qualified improvement property, or QIP, is the category that matters most to tenants. It is an improvement made to the interior of a nonresidential building that was already placed in service. The definition excludes three things: building enlargements, elevators and escalators, and the internal structural framework.

QIP has a 15-year recovery period and qualifies for bonus depreciation. It is also eligible for Section 179 expensing, along with roofs, HVAC, fire protection, alarm, and security systems on nonresidential property. Section 179 lets you deduct the cost immediately rather than over time, with a dollar limit that is above $1 million (the 2025 figure is $2.5 million) and a cap tied to your taxable income from active business.

So which do you use, bonus or Section 179? A few distinctions help:

1. **Section 179 is elective by asset and limited by income.** You cannot use it to create a loss from the business.
2. **Bonus depreciation is not limited by income.** It can create or increase a net operating loss.
3. **State rules differ.** California treats these two very differently, which matters for anyone filing in Tulare, Kings, Fresno, or Kern County.

Business owners who run an S-corp or LLC often have to plan between the two, because the loss limitations on their personal return affect the answer. If your build-out is large relative to your profit, a deduction you cannot use this year is not much of a benefit.

## How bonus depreciation timing changes the math

Bonus depreciation has been a moving target. Under the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, it was 100 percent through 2022, then stepped down by 20 points a year: 80 percent in 2023, 60 percent in 2024, and 40 percent in 2025 under that original schedule. Legislation signed in July 2025 restored 100 percent bonus for qualifying property acquired after January 19, 2025. Property acquired before that date falls under the earlier percentages.

That history matters for two reasons. First, the acquisition date, not just the in-service date, can determine your percentage. Second, whatever the current percentage is, any portion not taken as bonus is depreciated over the regular recovery period. At 60 percent bonus, for instance, a 15-year asset still produces a 60 percent first-year deduction plus a first-year slice of the remaining 40 percent.

The practical lesson is to model the numbers with the percentage that applies to your specific purchase or build-out date, and to check again before you sign a contract or take possession. Bonus percentages are the variable most likely to change between the time you plan and the time you file.

## Lease terms that decide who gets the deduction

For tenants, the lease is part of the tax plan. A few provisions are worth negotiating before you sign.

### Who pays and who owns

If you pay for the improvements, you generally depreciate them. If the landlord pays through a tenant improvement allowance, the landlord generally depreciates them, and the treatment on your side depends on how the lease is written. A mixed arrangement, where the landlord covers part of the build-out and you cover the rest, requires clear records of which dollars went where.

### Invoice detail

Ask your contractor to break out costs by trade and component instead of billing a lump sum. Itemized invoices are what make 5-, 7-, and 15-year classifications defensible later.

### Lease term and early exit

Improvements are generally recovered over their tax recovery period, not the length of your lease. If the lease ends early and you abandon improvements you paid for, the remaining basis may be deductible, subject to the facts. That is worth knowing if you are signing a five-year lease with a ten-year build-out.

### Triple-net obligations

Under a triple-net lease, tenants often pay for property taxes, insurance, and maintenance, and sometimes for replacements of building systems. Those payments can create depreciable assets, or they can be currently deductible repairs. Which one applies is an accounting question worth settling before the invoice arrives. The [December business audit](/resources/the-december-business-admin-audit) is a good moment to review lease obligations with that in mind.

## When you own the building and run the business in it

Many Aurora clients have two sides to the same problem. An owner holds the building in one LLC, and an operating company rents space from it. That raises a sequence of structural questions:

- Which entity pays for the build-out, and which entity owns it for tax purposes?
- Is rent set at a market rate and documented in a written lease?
- Does the intercompany balance get tracked, or does it quietly turn into a mess at year-end?

A build-out paid for by the operating company can be depreciated there, and a build-out paid for by the landlord entity can be depreciated there. Neither is automatically better. The right answer depends on which entity can use the deduction, which has passive-loss issues, and how state taxes apply. For clients with several entities or properties across states, this is where multi-state and multi-entity planning stops being a theoretical topic.

## Passive loss and material participation limits

A large first-year deduction is only useful if you can use it. Rental real estate losses are generally passive, which means they offset passive income, not wages or business profit, unless an exception applies.

The main exceptions:

- **Real estate professional status.** You must spend more than 750 hours a year in real property trades or businesses, more than half your personal service time must be in them, and you must materially participate in the rental activity. A W-2 employee with a full-time job elsewhere will rarely qualify.
- **The $25,000 allowance.** Taxpayers who actively participate in rental real estate can deduct up to $25,000 of losses against other income, but the allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 of modified adjusted gross income.
- **Short-term rental treatment.** A property with an average guest stay of seven days or less may not be treated as a rental activity at all, and material participation then decides the outcome.

Self-rental has its own trap. When you rent a building to a business you materially participate in, net rental income is treated as nonpassive, but a net loss stays passive. A large cost segregation deduction on a self-rented building can therefore produce a suspended loss on one side and no offset on the other. A grouping election may change that, so this is a planning conversation to have before the study, not after.

Suspended losses are not lost. They carry forward and are released when you have passive income or dispose of the property. But a deduction you cannot use for ten years is worth much less than one you can use now.

## California and other states may not follow the federal rules

Federal treatment is only half the picture for anyone filing in California. The state has historically not conformed to federal bonus depreciation, and its Section 179 limit has been far below the federal limit (the figure has been $25,000, with a phase-out that begins at a much lower level). Check the current figures with your CPA before relying on either.

The result is a split schedule. You take the accelerated deduction federally, then keep a separate California depreciation schedule that uses regular MACRS lives without bonus. A study still helps at the state level, because 5-, 7-, and 15-year property depreciates faster than 39-year property even without bonus. But the benefit is smaller, and a California state return that does not reflect the difference will produce errors and notices.

Anyone with properties or operations in several states faces more of this. Each state has its own conformity position, and tracking them is bookkeeping work that has to happen every year, not just in the study year.

## What happens when you sell

Accelerated depreciation does not disappear at sale. It comes back as recapture.

- **Personal property components (5- and 7-year).** Gain up to the depreciation you claimed is recaptured as ordinary income under Section 1245.
- **Real property components.** Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain is taxed at up to 25 percent.
- **Land improvements and QIP.** These can fall on either side of that line depending on classification and whether bonus was taken, so your CPA should model the specific components.

The usual way to defer or soften recapture is a like-kind exchange under Section 1031, or holding the property for the long term. Neither is automatic, and neither removes the need to model the exit before you take the deduction. If you expect to sell within two or three years with no exchange planned, the recapture can erase much of the benefit.

## What a study costs and when to skip it

Engineering-based studies for small and mid-size commercial properties commonly cost roughly $5,000 to $15,000, and more for large or complex buildings. Fees vary by firm, property type, and depth of analysis, so treat that range as a starting point and get quotes.

A study is usually worth it when:

1. The depreciable basis is roughly $500,000 or more.
2. You have enough taxable income, or qualifying passive income, to use the deductions.
3. You plan to hold the property or are doing a deferral strategy.
4. The build-out or acquisition is recent enough that the bonus percentage is meaningful.

It is usually not worth it when:

- The building is small, old, or mostly land value.
- You have a net operating loss or suspended passive losses with no way to absorb more.
- You will sell soon and cannot defer the recapture.
- California is your only state and the federal benefit is mostly locked behind passive limits.

A study that saves $40,000 in tax and costs $9,000 is a good deal. A study whose deduction sits suspended for a decade is not.

## Common Questions

**Can a tenant do a cost segregation study?**

Yes, if the tenant paid for the improvements. The study classifies the build-out costs into 5-, 7-, and 15-year property, much of which qualifies for bonus depreciation. Tenants cannot depreciate the building shell. For smaller build-outs, itemized invoices and a CPA's analysis may capture most of the benefit without a formal engineering study.

**Is qualified improvement property eligible for bonus depreciation?**

Yes. Qualified improvement property is a 15-year asset made to the interior of an existing nonresidential building, and it qualifies for bonus depreciation. It excludes enlargements, elevators and escalators, and internal structural framework. The bonus percentage depends on when the property was acquired and placed in service, so confirm the current rate before planning.

**Does cost segregation create a tax problem when I sell?**

It can. Depreciation taken on 5- and 7-year components is recaptured as ordinary income up to the amount claimed, and real property depreciation can be taxed at up to 25 percent. A 1031 exchange or a long hold can defer or reduce that cost, so model the exit before you commit to a study.
