A business owner in Visalia with a full calendar of clients can post a tidy year on paper and still be moving money between accounts to cover payroll or a quarterly tax payment. Nothing is wrong with the business. The customers are happy, the work keeps coming, and the books show a profit. The trouble is timing. Profit is how the year looks in a spreadsheet. Cash is what is in the checking account on the day a bill comes due.

This post walks through how to map where cash moves in your business, where it tends to get stuck, and how to schedule the financial checkups that catch a problem while it is still a small one. The numbers below are illustrative, not drawn from a specific client, but the patterns are ones business owners in Tulare, Kings, and Fresno counties run into every year.

## Profit is the paper, cash is the pulse

Business accounting has a built-in gap between what the tax return says and what the bank says. Some items reduce taxable income without costing a dime. Others cost real money and never show up as a deduction.

| Item | Shows up as a deduction? | Leaves your bank account? |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation on equipment or vehicles | Yes | No |
| Loan interest | Yes | Yes |
| Loan principal | No | Yes |
| New equipment or a build-out (a capital purchase) | Over time, through depreciation | Yes, all at once |
| Customer deposit received for future work | No, it is a liability | Comes in, but is not yet earned |
| Estimated tax payments | No | Yes |

Here is what that gap looks like. Say you run a service business in Visalia with $480,000 in annual revenue and $410,000 in operating expenses: payroll and contractors ($270,000), rent and utilities ($48,000), insurance and software ($42,000), and marketing and other costs ($50,000). Your books show about $70,000 of profit. But loan principal ($24,000), new equipment ($18,000), and estimated taxes ($14,000) are not operating expenses, so only about $14,000 is left in the bank. Depreciation on that equipment could shrink your taxable profit even further, while the bank account shows a modest surplus.

Both numbers are correct. Only one of them pays the bills.

## Map how cash moves through your business

Before you can find a blockage, you need to see the circulation. For most businesses, that means tracing four paths.

**Cash in.** Customer payments are the obvious source, but also count retainers, deposits, reimbursed expenses, and any other income. Note the day each customer actually pays, not the day the invoice is due. If your largest clients pay 45 days after you bill them, your cash is thinner than your sales suggest.

**Fixed cash out.** Payroll, rent, loan payments, insurance, and software subscriptions. These are predictable, so they set your floor.

**Lumpy cash out.** Annual insurance renewals, sales tax and payroll tax deposits, license fees, equipment replacements, and seasonal purchases. These are predictable in category but not in timing, which is where most trouble starts.

**Tax cash out.** Quarterly estimated payments, plus any balance due in April. The [IRS small business and self-employed resources](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) outline the estimated tax basics, and the quarterly dates generally fall on April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15.

If you run more than one entity, add a fifth path: money moving between them. A shared operating account that covers a bill at one company with revenue from another is convenient, but it blurs the books and makes it hard to see which entity is carrying its weight. Each entity should show its own cash position, and any transfers should be recorded as loans or contributions, not left unexplained.

## Find the blockages before they cut off circulation

In most businesses, clots rarely look dramatic. They look like a reasonable bill arriving at an awkward time.

### Payroll and tax deposits

Payroll lands on the same dates whether or not customers have paid. In our example, about $22,500 a month goes to payroll and contractors, and payroll tax deposits and sales tax remittances add to it on their own schedules. If your balance is thin the week before a deposit is due, the problem is not the business, it is the calendar. Every one of those dates needs a line on the forecast.

Annual bills deserve the same treatment. Insurance renewals, software contracts, and licenses often arrive once a year, and owners who budget only month to month are often surprised.

### Late-paying customers and slow seasons

One $15,000 invoice paid 30 days late can mean funding payroll from your own pocket, because your expenses still come due on time. A slow season works the same way: a 20 percent dip on $40,000 of monthly revenue is $8,000, more than half of the $14,000 surplus in our example. Customer deposits you are holding are not a cushion either. They are obligations for work you still owe.

### Equipment and capital purchases

A $9,000 equipment upgrade does not behave like a $900 repair. The upgrade is generally a capital purchase, depreciated over time, while the repair is typically a current deduction. The cash leaves the same way either way, but your tax return treats them very differently. Our post on [repairs versus capital improvements](/resources/understanding-the-difference-between-repairs-and-capital-imp) explains the tests that decide which bucket a purchase falls into, and that decision changes both your tax bill and your cash planning.

### Surprise tax bills

Selling a business asset, a one-time windfall, or simply a stronger year than expected can create taxable income the bank balance does not reflect. Depreciation recapture on a sale is the classic version: the deduction you enjoyed for years comes back as tax, and the cash is needed within months. If a big year is coming, a tax projection early enough to act on gives you room to plan.

### Debt resets and balloon dates

A variable-rate loan that resets, a line of credit up for renewal, or a term loan with a balloon can turn a comfortable cash position into a negative one overnight. These dates belong on the forecast the day you sign the loan, not the month the lender's notice arrives.

### Out-of-state and multi-entity friction

Hiring a remote employee or selling to customers in another state alongside your California operations? Each state can carry its own registration, filing, and tax obligations, and each entity may have its own bank account, insurance policy, and compliance calendar. More moving parts means more places for cash to sit unnoticed or for a filing fee to arrive unplanned. Many of our multi-state and multi-entity clients keep a single calendar showing every entity's due dates next to its cash balance.

## Schedule checkups so a crisis never gets the first word

A doctor does not wait for chest pain to check your blood pressure. Cash works the same way. Three recurring reviews catch most problems early.

| Checkup | Frequency | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly cash review | Every month, after bank reconciliation | Did the business perform the way we expected? |
| 13-week cash flow forecast | Updated every month, looking one quarter ahead | Will any week dip below my cash reserve? |
| Quarterly tax projection | Before each estimated payment date | What do I owe for the year so far, and what should I set aside? |

### The monthly review

Start with reconciled books. If last month's bank activity has not been matched to the ledger, every number downstream is a guess. Then compare revenue collected, expenses paid, and cash left over against what you expected. A service line that nets $1,200 a month when you expected $4,000 is telling you something: a cost increase, a pricey repeat expense, or a customer paying late.

### The 13-week forecast

This is the most useful tool most business owners do not have. List every expected deposit and payment by week for the next 13 weeks: customer payments, payroll dates, tax deposits, insurance renewals, a planned equipment purchase. Then watch the low point. If your lowest balance in the quarter is $6,000 and a slow month costs $8,000, you have found the clot with weeks to spare, and you can adjust before it becomes a late fee or a credit card balance.

A 13-week view also keeps you honest about reserves. A common rule of thumb is three to six months of fixed expenses, and the right number rises with how uneven your revenue is and how old your equipment is.

### The quarterly tax projection

Quarterly estimated payments should reflect this year's numbers, not last year's. A projection built from your actual revenue, expenses, and depreciation tells you whether to pay more, pay less, or move up a planned purchase. Small business tax planning done in March and June costs less, in money and stress, than a surprise in April. The projection also catches entity-level questions early, such as whether an S-corp election still fits the way your business has grown.

## Common Questions

**What is a 13-week cash flow forecast for a rental portfolio?**

It is a week-by-week projection of every deposit and payment over the next quarter, including rents, mortgage drafts, property tax installments, insurance renewals, and planned repairs. The goal is to find the lowest balance in the period, so you can fix a shortfall before it happens.

**Can a rental show a tax loss and still produce positive cash flow?**

Yes. Depreciation reduces taxable income without any cash leaving your account, so a property can show a paper loss while the bank balance grows. The reverse also happens: mortgage principal and capital repairs drain cash without appearing as deductions, so profit on paper can coexist with a thin bank balance.

**How often should a real estate investor review cash flow?**

Review actuals monthly, update the 13-week forecast at the same time, and run a tax projection before each quarterly estimate date. Add a review whenever you buy, sell, refinance, or hit a loan reset. Waiting for the annual tax return means you learn about problems 12 months too late.
