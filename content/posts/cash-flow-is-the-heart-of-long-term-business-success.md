A landlord in Visalia with four rentals can post a tidy year on paper and still be moving money between accounts to cover the November property tax installment. Nothing is wrong with the properties. The tenants pay, the houses stay full, and the books show a profit or a small paper loss. The trouble is timing. Profit is how the year looks in a spreadsheet. Cash is what is in the checking account on the day a bill comes due.

This post walks through how to map where cash moves in a rental portfolio, where it tends to get stuck, and how to schedule the financial checkups that catch a problem while it is still a small one. The numbers below are illustrative, not drawn from a specific client, but the patterns are ones real estate investors in Tulare, Kings, and Fresno counties run into every year.

## Profit is the paper, cash is the pulse

Rental accounting has a built-in gap between what the tax return says and what the bank says. Some items reduce taxable income without costing a dime. Others cost real money and never show up as a deduction.

| Item | Shows up as a deduction? | Leaves your bank account? |
|---|---|---|
| Depreciation (27.5 years for residential rentals) | Yes | No |
| Mortgage interest | Yes | Yes |
| Mortgage principal | No | Yes |
| New roof or HVAC (a capital improvement) | Over time, through depreciation | Yes, all at once |
| Tenant security deposit received | No, it is a liability | Comes in, but is not yours to spend |
| Estimated tax payments | No | Yes |

Here is what that gap looks like. Say you own four single-family rentals in Visalia, each renting for $1,900 a month, with a $950 monthly mortgage payment apiece. Annual rent is $91,200. After mortgage payments ($45,600), property taxes ($16,800), insurance ($6,000), and routine repairs ($7,000), you have about $15,800 left. Depreciation on those four houses could push your tax return to a small loss, while the bank account shows a modest surplus.

Both numbers are correct. Only one of them pays the bills.

## Map how cash moves through your rentals

Before you can find a blockage, you need to see the circulation. For a rental portfolio, that means tracing four paths.

**Cash in.** Rent is the obvious source, but also count pet fees, late fees, laundry income, and reimbursed utilities. Note the day each tenant actually pays, not the day rent is due. If three of four tenants pay on the 5th, your first week of every month is thinner than the lease suggests.

**Fixed cash out.** Mortgage payments, insurance, property management fees, and landscaping. These are predictable, so they set your floor.

**Lumpy cash out.** Property tax installments, insurance renewals, turnover costs, and capital repairs. These are predictable in category but not in timing, which is where most trouble starts.

**Tax cash out.** Quarterly estimated payments, plus any balance due in April. The [IRS small business and self-employed resources](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed) outline the estimated tax basics, and the 2025 dates fall on April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15.

If you hold properties in separate LLCs, add a fifth path: money moving between entities. A shared operating account that covers a repair at Property B with rent from Property A is convenient, but it blurs the books and makes it hard to see which property is carrying its weight. Each entity should show its own cash position, and any transfers should be recorded as loans or contributions, not left unexplained.

## Find the blockages before they cut off circulation

In a rental business, clots rarely look dramatic. They look like a reasonable bill arriving at an awkward time.

### Property tax installments

In California, the first installment of the property tax bill is due November 1 and becomes delinquent after December 10. The second is due February 1 and delinquent after April 10. On our four-house example, with $4,200 a year per property, that is $8,400 leaving your account twice a year. If your lender escrows taxes, the same money arrives as a higher monthly payment after an escrow shortage. Either way, it needs a line on the forecast.

Buying a property adds another wrinkle. A purchase triggers reassessment, and the supplemental tax bill can arrive months after closing. Investors who budget only for the seller's old tax number are often surprised.

### Vacancy and turnover

One five-week vacancy on a $1,900 rental costs about $2,200 in lost rent. Add $1,500 for paint, cleaning, and a carpet patch, and the mortgage still comes due on time. That is roughly $3,700 for a single turnover, which is close to a quarter of the $15,800 surplus in our example. California also requires security deposits to be returned or itemized within 21 days of move-out, so the deposit you were holding is not a cushion. It is a short-term obligation.

### Capital repairs

A $9,000 roof does not behave like a $900 plumbing fix. The roof is generally a capital improvement, depreciated over time, while the plumbing repair is typically a current deduction. The cash leaves the same way either way, but your tax return treats them very differently. Our post on [repairs versus capital improvements](/resources/understanding-the-difference-between-repairs-and-capital-imp) explains the tests that decide which bucket a project falls into, and that decision changes both your tax bill and your cash planning.

### Surprise tax bills

Selling a property, converting a depreciated rental, or having a strong year in a side business can create taxable income the bank balance does not reflect. Depreciation recapture on a sale is the classic version: the deduction you enjoyed for years comes back as tax, and the cash is needed within months. If you are weighing accelerated depreciation, [cost segregation](/resources/cost-segregation-for-business-owners-how-to-benefit-whether) can speed up deductions and improve near-term cash, but it should be modeled against what happens when you eventually sell.

### Debt resets and balloon dates

An adjustable-rate loan that resets, or a five-year commercial loan with a balloon, can turn a comfortable cash position into a negative one overnight. These dates belong on the forecast the day you sign the loan, not the month the lender's notice arrives.

### Out-of-state and multi-entity friction

Own a rental in Arizona or Texas alongside your California houses? Each state can carry its own registration, filing, and tax obligations, and each entity may have its own bank account, insurance policy, and compliance calendar. More moving parts means more places for cash to sit unnoticed or for a filing fee to arrive unplanned. Many of our real estate investor clients with multi-state holdings keep a single calendar showing every entity's due dates next to its cash balance.

## Schedule checkups so a crisis never gets the first word

A doctor does not wait for chest pain to check your blood pressure. Cash works the same way. Three recurring reviews catch most problems early.

| Checkup | Frequency | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly cash review | Every month, after bank reconciliation | Did each property perform the way we expected? |
| 13-week cash flow forecast | Updated every month, looking one quarter ahead | Will any week dip below my reserve? |
| Quarterly tax projection | Before each estimated payment date | What do I owe for the year so far, and what should I set aside? |

### The monthly review

Start with reconciled books. If last month's bank activity has not been matched to the ledger, every number downstream is a guess. Then compare each property's rent collected, expenses paid, and cash left over. A house that nets $120 a month when you expected $400 is telling you something: a rate increase, a pricey repeat repair, or a tenant paying late.

### The 13-week forecast

This is the most useful tool most investors do not have. List every expected deposit and payment by week for the next 13 weeks: rent dates, mortgage drafts, the November 1 property tax installment, insurance renewals, a planned repaint. Then watch the low point. If your lowest balance in the quarter is $2,000 and a turnover costs $3,700, you have found the clot with weeks to spare, and you can adjust before it becomes a late fee or a credit card balance.

A 13-week view also keeps you honest about reserves. A common rule of thumb is three to six months of mortgage, tax, and insurance payments per property, and the right number rises with the age of the roofs and HVAC systems you own.

### The quarterly tax projection

Quarterly estimated payments should reflect this year's numbers, not last year's. A projection built from your actual rent, expenses, and depreciation tells you whether to pay more, pay less, or move up a planned purchase. Small business tax planning done in March and June costs less, in money and stress, than a surprise in April. For investors, the projection also catches entity-level questions early, such as whether an LLC or S-corp election still fits the way your holdings have grown.

## Common Questions

**What is a 13-week cash flow forecast for a rental portfolio?**

It is a week-by-week projection of every deposit and payment over the next quarter, including rents, mortgage drafts, property tax installments, insurance renewals, and planned repairs. The goal is to find the lowest balance in the period, so you can fix a shortfall before it happens.

**Can a rental show a tax loss and still produce positive cash flow?**

Yes. Depreciation reduces taxable income without any cash leaving your account, so a property can show a paper loss while the bank balance grows. The reverse also happens: mortgage principal and capital repairs drain cash without appearing as deductions, so profit on paper can coexist with a thin bank balance.

**How often should a real estate investor review cash flow?**

Review actuals monthly, update the 13-week forecast at the same time, and run a tax projection before each quarterly estimate date. Add a review whenever you buy, sell, refinance, or hit a loan reset. Waiting for the annual tax return means you learn about problems 12 months too late.
