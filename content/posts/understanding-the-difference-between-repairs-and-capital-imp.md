It is January in Visalia, the tule fog has settled in, and the heater at your office is blowing cold air. Two weeks and $9,800 later you have a new system, and a question waiting for you at tax time: is that a deduction this year, or something you write off slowly over 39 years? The answer changes your tax bill by thousands of dollars, and the IRS tangible property regulations do not make it obvious. Most business owners we meet are making these calls one invoice at a time, usually after the work is done, and usually by gut feel.

This guide gives you a working framework. You will see the three tests the IRS applies, the safe harbors that settle most small purchases without any debate, worked examples from a typical Central Valley business, and a checklist you can run before the next contractor invoice lands.

## Why the line between repair and improvement costs business owners real money

A repair keeps property in ordinary working condition. You deduct it in the year you pay for it. A capital improvement adds value, extends life, or adapts the property to a new use. You add it to the property's basis and recover it through depreciation, which for a commercial building you own means 39 years, and for equipment, vehicles, and many other business assets means shorter recovery periods.

Here is what that difference looks like on the HVAC replacement above. At a combined federal and California rate of around 35 percent, expensing $9,800 saves roughly $3,430 in tax this year. Capitalizing the same cost over 39 years produces a deduction of about $251 a year, which is worth roughly $88 in annual tax savings. Same invoice, same building, same business. The timing alone moves thousands of dollars of cash.

The risk runs both ways. Deduct something that should have been capitalized and you create audit exposure and a possible amended return. Capitalize something that could have been expensed and you hand the IRS an interest-free loan for decades. The [IRS tangible property final regulations](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/tangible-property-final-regulations) were written to reduce that guesswork, and once you know the structure they are more workable than their reputation suggests.

## Start with the unit of property

Before you can ask whether work is a repair, you have to know what you are repairing. The regulations call this the unit of property, and it matters because the tests compare your spending against that unit, not against the whole building.

For a building, the unit of property is the building structure plus nine separate building systems: HVAC, plumbing, electrical, escalators, elevators, fire protection and alarm, security, gas distribution, and other structural-support systems. Each system is tested on its own. Replacing the roof is measured against the building structure. Replacing a condenser, compressor, or air handler is measured against the HVAC system.

Each building you own is generally its own unit of property, so your office and a warehouse across town are tested separately. Equipment works similarly: a machine, a vehicle, or a connected computer system is generally tested as one unit. This also means a $9,800 HVAC replacement is not judged as a small slice of your whole business. It is judged against the system it replaced.

## The three tests: betterment, adaptation, restoration

Under the regulations, you must capitalize an amount if it results in a betterment, an adaptation, or a restoration of the unit of property. Practitioners call this the BAR test. If the spending fits none of the three, it is generally a deductible repair. [IRS Publication 535](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p535) covers the deductible side of that line.

### Betterment

Spending is a betterment if it does any of the following:

- Fixes a defect that existed before you acquired the property.
- Materially adds to the property through enlargement, expansion, or extension.
- Materially increases capacity, productivity, efficiency, strength, or quality.

The first item catches new building owners by surprise. If you buy a 1970s commercial building in Visalia with a leaking roof deck or a failing sewer lateral and fix it after closing, that is not a repair, even though the work looks like one. The defect predates you, so the cost is capitalized.

The efficiency trigger matters when you upgrade. Swapping a handful of broken windows for matching ones is generally a repair. Replacing every window in the building with higher-grade dual-pane units, so energy performance improves materially, leans toward a betterment.

### Adaptation

Spending is an adaptation if it converts the property to a use that differs from its intended ordinary use when you placed it in service. Converting a storage area into a customer showroom is a common example. So is turning a retail suite into professional office space. The cost is capitalized, and you also start a new depreciation schedule for the converted space.

### Restoration

Restoration is where most disputes live. Spending is a restoration if it:

- Replaces a major component or a substantial structural part of the unit of property.
- Rebuilds the property to a like-new condition after the end of its economic useful life.
- Returns the property to ordinary working condition after it fell into disrepair and stopped functioning.
- Repairs damage from a casualty event for which you took a loss deduction.
- Follows a partial disposition where you deducted the remaining basis of the replaced part.

A full roof replacement is the standard example. The roof is a major component of the building structure, so replacing it is a restoration. Patching a leaking section is not.

## Three safe harbors that settle most of the small stuff

The regulations include three safe harbors that let you skip the BAR analysis for a large share of ordinary spending. Each has its own conditions, and two of them require an election on your return.

### De minimis safe harbor election

The de minimis safe harbor election lets you expense items up to $2,500 per invoice or per item, if you do not have audited financial statements. With them, the threshold is $5,000. The election is made annually by attaching a statement to your timely filed original return, including extensions, and it cannot be added later on an amended return. You also need to treat these amounts as expenses on your books, so your bookkeeping has to match your tax position.

For most business owners, this is the workhorse. A $1,400 water heater, a $2,100 piece of shop equipment, or a $900 office printer falls under it without a debate over whether the item is a restoration. The election applies to all qualifying items for the year, so it is an all-or-nothing choice.

### Routine maintenance safe harbor

The routine maintenance safe harbor covers recurring work you reasonably expect to perform more than once during the property's class life. For buildings, that window is 10 years. It requires no election, but the expectation has to be reasonable based on your experience and industry practice.

Exterior repainting every five or six years qualifies. So does re-caulking, resealing a parking area, or servicing HVAC equipment on a schedule. A water heater you replace every 12 years does not, because you would not do it twice in the window. This safe harbor also does not apply to work that is a betterment, or to repairs done as part of a larger improvement.

### Small taxpayer safe harbor for buildings

The small taxpayer safe harbor for buildings is the one many Visalia business owners who own their building qualify for and never use. It applies when your average annual gross receipts for the prior three years are $10 million or less, and the building's unadjusted basis is $1 million or less. If your total repairs, maintenance, and improvement spending on that building for the year is no more than the lesser of $10,000 or 2 percent of the unadjusted basis, you can deduct all of it.

Take a small commercial building bought for $380,000, with $60,000 allocated to land. The building's unadjusted basis is $320,000, and 2 percent of that is $6,400. That is your cap for the year on that building, and everything spent on it must fit under that number to qualify. Spend $6,300 and it all becomes deductible, including work that would otherwise have been a restoration. Spend $6,500 and the safe harbor is off the table for that building. The election is made building by building, each year, on a statement attached to the return.

## Worked examples from a typical Visalia business

These are illustrative scenarios, not advice for your specific situation, but they show how the tests and safe harbors interact.

| Spending | Likely treatment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Patch three shingle sections after a leak, $1,800 | Deduct as a repair | Keeps the roof in working condition without replacing a major part |
| Full roof replacement on your commercial building, $14,500 | Capitalize over 39 years | Restoration of a major component of the building structure |
| HVAC capacitor and refrigerant recharge, $650 | Deduct as a repair | Maintains the existing system |
| Whole HVAC system replacement, $9,800 | Capitalize over 39 years | Replaces an entire building system |
| Water heater replacement, $1,400 | Deduct under the de minimis safe harbor | Under the $2,500 per item threshold |
| Exterior repaint of your shop or storefront every five years, $4,200 | Deduct under routine maintenance | Recurring work expected more than once in 10 years |
| Converting storage space into a customer showroom, $48,000 | Capitalize and start a new schedule | Adaptation to a new use |
| Engine rebuild on a company truck used for deliveries and job sites, $6,500 | Capitalize | Replaces a major component of the vehicle |

### Roof patch versus roof replacement

A patch is a repair because it keeps the roof doing what it already does. A replacement is a capital cost. But there is a useful second step: the partial disposition election. When you replace a roof, you can elect to treat the old roof as disposed of and deduct its remaining undepreciated basis. That requires estimating what part of the original building cost belonged to the roof, and a cost segregation study can support the number. On older properties where the roof was never separately tracked, this election can recover a deduction that would otherwise sit buried in the building's basis.

### HVAC repair versus new system

Central Valley summers regularly push past 100 degrees and winter mornings turn cold and foggy, so HVAC spending is constant for Visalia business owners. A service call is a repair. A complete system replacement is a restoration of a building system. The gray zone is a partial replacement, such as a new condenser or compressor on an otherwise working system. The test is whether you replaced a major component or a substantial part of the system, which depends on the cost and function of the part relative to the whole. Document the reasoning when the call is close.

### Vehicle engine overhaul

If your business runs a truck for deliveries, job sites, or client visits, the same rules apply to it. Oil changes, brakes, and tires are repairs. A full engine rebuild or replacement restores a major component and is capitalized. If you are weighing whether to repair an older truck or replace it, our post on [auto leasing versus buying](/resources/auto-leasing-vs-buying-which-makes-more-sense) walks through how depreciation and Section 179 change that math.

## Costs you capitalize even when they look like repairs

Two rules trip up business owners who think of themselves as only doing repairs.

The first is the acquisition rule. Costs you pay to get a newly purchased property ready for business are generally part of the acquisition cost. If you buy a building in Tulare County, replace the flooring, repaint everything, and fix the plumbing before you open your doors, those costs belong in basis, not on the repairs line. The same work after the property has been in service can be a deductible repair.

The second is the improvement-related repair rule. When you do a real improvement, the repairs that directly benefit it, or that happen because of it, are capitalized with the project. If you remodel your office and patch the drywall and repaint the walls as part of that job, the patching and painting join the remodel's cost. The same drywall patch done alone, after a plumbing leak, is a repair. Mixed invoices from contractors should be itemized, because a lump-sum bill makes it hard to defend any split.

## What to do once you capitalize

Capitalized does not always mean slow. Depending on what you spent on, [IRS Publication 946](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p946) shows several routes to speed up the deduction.

- **Bonus depreciation.** It applies to property with a recovery period of 20 years or less. In a business, that includes equipment, machinery, furniture, vehicles, and land improvements such as fencing, paving, and landscaping. It does not reach the building itself or its structural components. For property acquired after January 19, 2025, 100 percent bonus depreciation is available again under recent legislation, so confirm the rules for the year of purchase.
- **Section 179.** It can cover business equipment and, for nonresidential buildings such as a retail space or small office, qualified real property improvements including roofs, HVAC, fire protection, and security systems. Limits and income rules apply, so confirm what you qualify for before relying on it.
- **Cost segregation.** A study separates a building's cost into shorter-lived components that qualify for faster depreciation. Our guide to [cost segregation for business owners](/resources/cost-segregation-for-business-owners-how-to-benefit-whether) explains when it is worth the fee.

California adds a wrinkle. The state does not conform to federal bonus depreciation, and it caps Section 179 at $25,000. A Visalia business owner who takes a large federal first-year deduction will carry a separate, slower California depreciation schedule for the same asset. That is manageable, but only if someone is tracking both.

## What misclassification costs

The consequences show up in a few forms. Overly aggressive expensing can lead to adjusted returns, interest, and accuracy penalties if an exam finds that a $14,500 roof was deducted in a single year. Over-capitalizing is quieter but still expensive. You lose deductions for years, and if you sell the asset, unclaimed depreciation is generally treated as taken anyway, so you pay recapture tax on a benefit you never received.

If you have been classifying things inconsistently, the fix is usually not a pile of amended returns. An automatic accounting method change filed on Form 3115 lets you correct your approach and take any catch-up adjustment in a single year. When prior years were too conservative, that can produce a meaningful deduction in the year of the change.

## A decision checklist for the next invoice

Run each large expense through these steps in order.

1. **Identify the unit of property.** Is this the building structure, a specific building system, or equipment like a vehicle?
2. **Check the acquisition rule.** Did you buy the property recently, and is this work getting it ready for business? If yes, capitalize.
3. **Check the de minimis safe harbor.** Is the invoice or item at or under $2,500? If you made the election, expense it.
4. **Check routine maintenance.** Will you reasonably do this same work more than once in 10 years? If yes, and it is not a betterment, expense it.
5. **Run the BAR tests.** Does it fix a pre-existing defect, add capacity, convert the use, or replace a major component? If yes, capitalize.
6. **Test the small taxpayer safe harbor.** Is total spending on this building for the year under the lesser of $10,000 or 2 percent of its unadjusted basis?
7. **Look for acceleration.** For capitalized costs, check whether any pieces qualify for bonus depreciation, Section 179, or a partial disposition election.
8. **Document it.** Keep the invoice, a short note on the reasoning, and a photo of the before condition for any close call.

## Common Questions

**Is a new roof a repair or a capital improvement?**

A full roof replacement is a capital improvement because it restores a major component of the building structure. For a commercial building you own, you depreciate it over 39 years. A patch that fixes a leak is a repair and is deducted immediately. You may also elect a partial disposition to write off the old roof's remaining basis.

**What is the de minimis safe harbor threshold?**

If you do not have audited financial statements, you can expense items costing $2,500 or less per invoice or per item. The threshold is $5,000 with audited statements. You must make the election each year with a statement attached to your timely filed original return, and your books must treat the amounts as expenses.

**Can I deduct a new HVAC system in the year I install it?**

It depends. A whole-system replacement is generally capitalized and depreciated, over 39 years for a commercial building you own. In a nonresidential building, Section 179 may allow a first-year deduction for HVAC, subject to its limits. If the cost is under the de minimis threshold or fits the small taxpayer safe harbor, you can expense it.

**What if I have misclassified repairs and improvements in past years?**

You generally do not need to amend every return. An automatic accounting method change on Form 3115 corrects the treatment going forward and lets you claim any catch-up deduction in the year of the change. Having a CPA or Enrolled Agent review your fixed asset schedule first shows how large the adjustment is.
